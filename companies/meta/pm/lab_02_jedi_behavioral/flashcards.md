## Card 1 — The 5 Jedi Dimensions

**Q:** Name the 5 Jedi dimensions that Meta evaluates in the behavioral round. For each, give a one-sentence behavioral definition — not a dictionary definition.

**A:**
1. **Resolve Conflict:** Went directly to the other person (no triangulation), named the disagreement explicitly, and found a resolution both parties could articulate.

2. **Grow Continuously:** Solicited specific feedback, changed observable behavior based on it within the next project, and can name the specific change a colleague would have seen.

3. **Embrace Ambiguity:** Started with partial information, stated assumptions explicitly with reversibility thinking, moved forward, and adapted when assumptions were wrong.

4. **Drive Results:** Defined a specific success metric BEFORE starting, tracked it weekly, and changed approach (not explanation) when the metric wasn't moving.

5. **Communicate Effectively:** Tailored message to the audience without prompting, delivered difficult messages directly without softening the substance, and confirmed understanding after.

## Card 2 — "Be Direct" vs Aggressive

**Q:** Meta's value "Be Direct" is one of the most commonly mentioned in Jedi interviews. What is the behavioral difference between "Be Direct" and being aggressive?

**A:**
**Be Direct:**
- Names the problem or disagreement explicitly without euphemism
- Gives the other person all the information they need to understand your position
- Leaves space for the other person to respond
- Focuses on the issue, not the person
- Done in private first (before escalation)
- Outcome: both parties know what was said and can act on it

**Aggressive:**
- Names the problem in a way that attacks the person's character or intelligence
- Uses emotional intensity or volume to win the argument
- Doesn't create space for the other person to respond
- Focuses on winning, not on resolving
- Can be done publicly or in front of others to gain leverage
- Outcome: one party is demoralized or shuts down

**Litmus test:** After a direct conversation, could both parties relay to a third party what was said and what was decided? If yes, it was direct. If the other party would describe the conversation as "being attacked" or "ambushed," it was aggressive, not direct.

## Card 3 — STAR with 4 Follow-Ups (Depth Requirement)

**Q:** Why does the Meta Jedi round go 3-5 follow-ups deep on each story? What is the interviewer testing that a single STAR answer doesn't reveal?

**A:** The Jedi follow-up depth tests three things a single STAR answer cannot show:

1. **Whether the story is real.** Real stories have specific details that emerge under questioning: "She initially said 'I don't understand why you're bringing this up now' — which told me the timing of my approach was the problem." Fabricated or over-polished stories run out of detail at the second follow-up.

2. **Whether you have genuine self-awareness.** "What would you do differently?" on a story that ended well is easy to dodge with "I think I handled it well." A good Jedi interviewer follows up: "What specifically would you change about how you approached the first conversation?" The answer reveals whether you've actually reflected on this experience.

3. **Whether the learning was real and changed your behavior.** "What changed in your behavior after the feedback?" requires describing something observable. "I became more aware" is not an answer. "I started sending a written summary of every decision made in a meeting within 24 hours — my manager mentioned in the next review that stakeholders were noting this" is an answer.

The depth rule: every Jedi story should sustain 4 follow-ups. If a story runs dry at the second follow-up, don't use it.

## Card 4 — Meta Values (6 Core)

**Q:** What are Meta's core values, and how should they appear in a Jedi interview without feeling like you're reciting a list?

**A:** Meta's core values (current as of 2024):
1. **Move Fast** — Ship quickly, iterate based on data, don't wait for perfect
2. **Be Direct** — Say what you think; give the full message even when uncomfortable
3. **Build Awesome Things** — Aim for genuine excellence, not incremental
4. **Live in the Future** — Think about what the world will need in 10 years
5. **Stay Focused** — Say no to most things to do a few things well
6. **Keep People Informed** — Transparency about progress, blockers, and decisions

**How to weave them in naturally:** Reference the value as an explanation for a choice, not as a label: "I knew the right thing to do was to address this directly with her — waiting would have let it fester and that's not how I want to work" is better than "I demonstrated 'Be Direct' in this situation." The value should be implicit in the behavior, not stated as a badge.

In the Conflict story: "Be Direct" shows in going to the person immediately.
In the Ambiguity story: "Move Fast" shows in starting with stated assumptions rather than waiting.
In the Drive Results story: "Stay Focused" shows in defining a narrow metric and not adding scope.

## Card 5 — "Grow Continuously" Behavioral Signature

**Q:** What does "Grow Continuously" look like in an interview story that convinces a skeptical Meta interviewer?

**A:** Three elements that distinguish a convincing Grow Continuously story from a generic one:

1. **The feedback was specific, not generic.** "My manager said I needed to communicate better" is generic. "My manager said that engineers were complaining that they didn't know where the project stood week-to-week — they were doing work that was already deprioritized" is specific. Specific feedback is credible; generic feedback sounds like a self-flattering story about how well you received criticism.

2. **Your initial reaction was not perfectly composed.** "When I first heard it, I was defensive — my first thought was 'I sent weekly emails, why don't they read them?' I caught that reaction and didn't say it." This is more credible than "I thanked my manager and immediately got to work on the improvement." Real growth involves a moment of resistance.

3. **The behavioral change is observable by others.** "I started running a weekly standup where I explicitly summarized: what we decided this week, what changed, what anyone should act on" — this is observable. "I became more mindful of communication" is not. The gold standard: a colleague or manager commented on the change without being prompted ("I noticed you've been much more structured in how you run meetings").

## Card 6 — Why Meta Goes 3-5 Follow-Ups Deep

**Q:** Why does the Meta Jedi round go deeper on fewer stories, while Amazon goes broader across more leadership principles? What does each format optimize for?

**A:**
**Amazon (LP breadth):** 14 Leadership Principles means the interview surface is wide. The format optimizes for: does this person have stories that map to each principle? It's easier to fake breadth by preparing a library of stories. But interviewers have limited time per principle, so depth isn't required.

**Meta (Jedi depth):** 5 dimensions with 3-5 follow-ups per story. The format optimizes for: is this person's behavior genuine and reflective, or is this a rehearsed performance? It's much harder to fake depth. By the 4th follow-up ("how did this affect the team dynamic 3 months later?"), a fabricated story runs dry.

**What this means for prep:**
- Amazon: prepare 2 stories per LP = 28 stories (breadth).
- Meta: prepare 1 deeply lived story per Jedi dimension = 5 stories, each with 4 follow-up answers prepared (depth).

The Meta approach requires fewer total stories but demands that each story be genuinely rich, personally owned, and lived enough that follow-up questions produce new information rather than restating the STAR.

## Card 7 — Quantified Result Format

**Q:** In the Drive Results dimension, what does a properly quantified result look like? Give an example of a weak result statement and a strong one for the same outcome.

**A:**
**Weak result:** "We shipped the feature on time and users responded positively."

Problems: "On time" — compared to what? "Positively" — how? What changed?

**Strong result:** "We shipped in 6 weeks, 2 weeks ahead of the original timeline. 30-day retention for the users who received the feature in the A/B test improved 8 percentage points vs. control (41% to 49%), with the effect holding at 90 days. The guardrail metric — support ticket volume — was flat."

What makes it strong:
- **Comparison baseline:** "2 weeks ahead of original timeline" vs. "on time" (compared to what?)
- **User-facing metric:** retention, not a feature metric (not "we shipped it")
- **Effect size AND comparison:** 8 percentage points, treatment vs. control
- **Time horizon:** "held at 90 days" shows the result wasn't a one-week spike
- **Guardrail included:** shows you were watching for harm, not just gain

In the Jedi round, "we shipped X and it went well" is a floor answer. The ceiling is being able to speak to the outcome metric you defined before starting, the effect size, and the time horizon.

## Card 8 — "Drive Results" vs "Bias for Action" (Meta vs Amazon)

**Q:** Amazon's "Bias for Action" and Meta's "Drive Results" both value moving quickly. How are they different, and how does the behavioral expression differ in an interview story?

**A:**
**Bias for Action (Amazon):** The emphasis is on *moving* — taking action despite uncertainty, accepting reversible mistakes as the cost of speed. The behavioral signal: "I started immediately rather than waiting for more information." The evaluation is: did you move when others were waiting?

**Drive Results (Meta):** The emphasis is on *outcomes* — defining the right metric upfront, tracking it, and changing approach when it's not moving. The behavioral signal: "I defined what success looked like before starting, tracked it weekly, and when it wasn't moving by week 3, I changed the approach rather than waiting and explaining." The evaluation is: did you achieve the thing that mattered, not just the thing you shipped?

**Key difference in an interview story:**
- A Bias for Action story can end with "I moved fast and we shipped it." (The action IS the result.)
- A Drive Results story must end with a metric: "We shipped it, and here's what it did to the thing we were trying to move." (The result is the result, not the action.)

**Interview implication:** In a Meta Jedi interview, if your Drive Results story ends when the feature ships and doesn't include what happened to the outcome metric you were optimizing, you've given a Bias for Action story — not a Drive Results story.
